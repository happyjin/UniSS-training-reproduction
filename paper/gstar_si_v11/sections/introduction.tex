\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A meeting interpreter renders each contribution into the listener's language and makes clear whose it is: a
``yes'' from the project manager means something different from a ``yes'' from the client. And people talk at once:
over a third of our AMI test windows~\citep{carletta2005ami} hold several speakers. Simultaneous speech-to-speech
translation (SimulS2ST) systems translate one speaker and can keep that speaker's
voice~\citep{seamless2023,labiausse2025hibiki,cheng2025liveinterpret,deng2025simuls2s}. DiariST~\citep{yang2024diarist}
streams speaker-attributed text translation of overlapping conversations, without timestamps or speech;
X-Translator~\citep{zhao2026xtranslator} routes each committed span of one recognition stream to one speaker. We are not
aware of a multi-speaker system that reports speech latency on overlapping meetings (Appendix~\ref{app:related7}).

Three gaps separate these systems from a meeting interpreter. \emph{Who said what}: during overlap, a speaker-blind
translator followed by speaker attribution keeps one speaker per span and loses the other voice. \emph{When to write}:
a streaming decoder must commit text before an utterance ends. \emph{When it is heard}: once speech is synthesised in
each speaker's voice, a block can only play after that speaker's earlier speech, so the listener's delay depends on
how long the generated speech is, not only on when its text was written.

We present \sys{} (\emph{Meeting Interpretation with Speaker Tracking}), in which one decoder writes, for every speaker
of a window, speaker-attributed, time-stamped translation segments $\spk{k}\,\tim{a}\,\text{translation}\,\tim{b}$,
which a speech generator speaks in the voice of speaker $k$ (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). The decoder reads a frozen
streaming diarizer, whose slots stay fixed for the whole meeting, as speaker tokens interleaved with its acoustic tokens
(adapted from G-STAR;~\citealp{peng2026gstar}), so two concurrent voices stay two speakers; it writes speaker first,
because a segment's voice must be known before its first word is spoken. It writes on deadlines, each chunk one fixed
lag after its source.

For the third gap we introduce \emph{playback alignment} (PA): the speech generator proposes several renderings of each
block, and a linear-chain CRF over the playback backlog scores them by their own likelihood, the fidelity of an
independent recogniser and the delay at which each word is heard; a latency budget is imposed by posterior
regularisation~\citep{ganchev2010posterior}, and the
generator is fine-tuned once on the posterior-weighted candidates. Backward messages carry the queueing cost of later
blocks into the choice for the current one. For the second gap we also tried the analogous latent-variable route,
\emph{write alignment}, which lets the read at which each chunk is written and the speaker order within a read be latent
and trains on their exact tempered posterior. Like other likelihood-trained latent
policies~\citep{arivazhagan2019milk,ma2020mma}, it drifts towards later writes, because more audio makes the same
translation more likely; we report it as an analysis.

Our contributions are (i) speaker-first, time-stamped joint decoding with the diarizer read inside the decoder, whose
gain on overlapped speech is mainly attribution; (ii) an overlap-aware evaluation of whole meetings run online, with
cascade and relabelling controls on the same diarizer, that separates when speech starts, when it ends, how fast it is
spoken and how often it pauses (\S\ref{sec:eval-overlap}); (iii) playback alignment, which on fixed text significantly
raises ASR-BLEU and lowers both conventional and playback-aware speech latency on three test sets
(\S\ref{sec:res-pa})\ifFactorial{}, with a factorial ablation of its parts\fi\ifAliEval{} and a test on unused meetings\fi; and (iv) an analysis of latent write alignment, which yields no significant gain, and of why
(\S\ref{sec:write}). Per utterance the system remains closer to consecutive than to simultaneous interpretation; we
therefore call it \emph{streaming}.
