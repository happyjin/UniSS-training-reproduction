\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sys{} writes who, when and what for every speaker of an overlapping window; reading the diarizer in the decoder helps
attribution where people talk at once. Playback alignment brings significant speech gains, largely
matched by a rule-based rate with candidate selection; write alignment brings none that survives correction. With one
output channel per speaker, a listener hears several translated voices at once during overlap; a single channel falls
far behind (Appendix~\ref{app:v9proto}).

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Test hygiene and missing controls.} The final streaming results are not fully blind
(\S\ref{sec:res-hygiene}): the write bias was added after a first test run, the stage-2 checkpoint deviates from the
selection rule and exceeds its latency bound, and the same test meetings served throughout development. We did not
decode the rule-selected checkpoint on test or evaluate on an untouched meeting set. We did not run the controls that
would attribute the text effect of write alignment: a write-bias sweep on the deadline baseline compared at matched
latency, deadline training continued for the same number of steps, and deadline training with mixed lags. We did not
isolate the speech-layer components (merging, rate rule, candidate selection, learned Talker) on the baseline text, and
the Zh$\rightarrow$En PA Talker was trained on outputs of a separately trained write-aligned Thinker, not on the
baseline's text. Fixed-lag, LocalAgreement, cascade and relabelling baselines were run only with the per-direction model under
the earlier look-ahead protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:v9proto}), not with the final speech layer. Every streaming model was
trained once, with one seed. The training-time latency budget was run with one value.

\paragraph{Latency.} LAAL$_\text{spread}$ is not the conventional speech LAAL; under the block-start convention the
gains largely disappear (Table~\ref{tab:m2laal10}), and LAAL may degenerate when, as here, most words are written at a
window's last read (Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). Latency differences are point estimates without intervals. Latencies
count audio and playback time, not computation; the decoder re-encodes the prefix at every read and the PA Talker
samples two candidates per block, and we did not measure the cost of the final system. All streaming latencies assume
one channel per speaker.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} No human evaluation: no listening test of voice identity, naturalness or comprehensibility, and
no bilingual check of the machine-translated En$\rightarrow$Zh references, which come from the same pipeline as the
training targets. No learned quality metric is used; tcpBLEU keeps duplicate translations of a unit and so partly rewards
duplication, and ASR-BLEU after CTC-based candidate selection may favour ASR-friendly speech. The Zh$\rightarrow$En
source is synthetic (re-voiced by the same model family as our Talker), the two AMI directions share their 16 meetings,
and on AliMeeting, the only real Mandarin set, the text score does not improve. AliMeeting audio is mixed close-talk
channels and we do not report DiariST's own metric, so the numbers are not comparable with published results; the reference windows keep
\num{v6:filt:en2zh:all:dur}\% of the En$\rightarrow$Zh test speech. Greedy bf16 decoding is not batch-invariant
(Appendix~\ref{app:noise}). Earlier studies left further tests unrun for compute (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}), and other
multi-speaker systems are compared by capability only (Table~\ref{tab:related8}).

\paragraph{System.} The diarizer, the encoder and the Thinker are trained separately from the Talker, and the two
alignments are not one joint model. Committed output is never revised, so a wrong speaker label is spoken. Voice prompts
come from the diarizer's solo regions, which a speaker who never talks alone lacks.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

Speaker-attributed interpretation processes the voices of meeting participants and reproduces them in another
language. Voice cloning can be misused to impersonate people; a deployed system should obtain the participants'
consent and mark synthesised speech as such. Our Chinese AMI test audio clones the voices of AMI participants into
Chinese and is synthetic speech. AMI is distributed under a CC BY 4.0 licence and was recorded with the participants'
consent; AliMeeting (CC BY-SA 4.0) is used for testing only.
